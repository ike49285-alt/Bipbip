Bipbip · evaluation method

Signal or Placebo

Bip is trained on nothing but raw candles and asked one question. Almost all the engineering isn't in the model — it's in the rules that stop a backtest flattering itself.

TQQQ 30-minute bars 48,973 bars, 2011–2026 27,491 minute bars, 70 sessions round trip 3.10 bps

The question we ask it

Every 30 minutes Bip is asked one thing: if I buy at the next bar's open and sell 30 minutes later, do I end up ahead? Yes or no. Not what the price will be — just up or down.

That yes/no is the label. We can compute it for every bar in history, because we already know what happened next. Forty-nine thousand bars, forty-nine thousand answers sitting in the back of the book.

What Bip actually is

A gradient-boosted tree is a tall stack of yes/no questions — Twenty Questions played badly, then repaired.

The first tree asks something crude: was the last bar's high more than 0.3% above its close? — then guesses. It is barely better than a coin. The second tree doesn't attempt the original question at all. It looks at where the first tree went wrong and predicts those mistakes. The third cleans up what's left.

Stack a few hundred and the pile can express genuinely intricate rules — when volume was thin three bars ago, and the last two candles closed near their lows, and it's early afternoon, lean short. Nobody writes those. They fall out of grinding through examples.

Since the indicators came off, it sees the last twelve candles: open, high, low, close, volume — 59 numbers. Everything divided by the current close, because TQQQ was $0.30 in 2011 and $71 today. Skip that and the tree learns "when price is near $5, do X", which is a rule about the year 2012, not about price.

Why training scores are worthless

Show Bip 10,000 bars with the answers attached and it builds its stack to get as many right as it can. It will always look brilliant on those 10,000 bars. Given enough trees it memorises them outright, noise included.

So nothing measured on training data counts. It's a student who has seen the answer key. Every number on this page comes from bars the model was never shown.

Time is not shuffleable

Ordinary machine learning shuffles the data and holds out a random 20% to test on. That is catastrophic here, and it is the easiest way in the field to fool yourself.

Shuffling puts Tuesday in the training set and Monday in the test set. They are not independent — same volatility regime, overlapping price moves. The model effectively studies tomorrow's exam. Results look superb and none of it survives a live market.

So we walk forward instead. Train on the past, test on the future that follows it, slide, repeat. Always in that direction.

Four rules that keep it honest

Each of these exists because breaking it manufactures a fake edge. Most of them were broken in this repo at some point, and every one of those bugs pointed the same way — toward good news.

Purge and embargo the fold boundary

A label for “the 30 minutes after 3:30pm” overlaps a test set starting at 3:31pm. Leave a gap of one full holding period between them.

One trade per holding period

Hold for 30 minutes but sample every minute and trade #2 is twenty-nine thirtieths the same trade as #1. Near-copies counted as independent evidence.

Cost of ignoring it: the t-statistic climbed 1.60 → 5.55 → 10.78 as the horizon lengthened — tracking √horizon exactly, on no new information at all.

Charge cost after choosing a direction

Pick long or short first, then subtract the spread. Netting it into the forward return beforehand looks equivalent and isn't.

Cost of ignoring it: with the sign inverted a short was paid 4.34 bps instead of charged it, producing “+8.58 bps edge at every hour of the day”.

Run the whole search again on nonsense

The control arm. Described below — it is the one that decides everything.

The placebo arm

Take the identical setup and shuffle the labels, so bar 400's answer is glued to bar 12,000's. There is now definitionally nothing to learn. Run the entire search again.

It still finds something. It always does. Any procedure flexible enough to discover a real pattern is flexible enough to hallucinate one, and shuffled data tells you how large that hallucination usually is.

A drug that cures 40% of patients sounds excellent until the sugar pill cures 40% too. So the number that actually matters is the difference:

what gets read

skill = real net − shuffled net

Both arms pay the same spread on every trade, so subtracting removes the cost and leaves only what the model knew.

Reading a learning curve

To ask how much data does it need, hold the test blocks fixed and vary only the training width. Slide both together and you confound “more data” with “a different market”.

The Net column looks dreadful — but the shuffled arm looks equally dreadful, because it pays the same spread. Six times the training data moves skill nowhere.

That is what a learning curve is: does skill climb as bars climbs? Here it doesn't — not over 6× at minutes, and not over 16× across the fifteen-year 30-minute archive.

What we could have detected

the arithmetic that settles it

2 × 1.41 spread + 0.278 SEC fee = 3.10 bps per round trip

A tradeable edge therefore has to clear roughly 5 bps. Detecting 5 bps at 30 minutes takes 76 sessions of held-out data. The archive holds 3,844.

So we have around fifty times the data required to find a tradeable edge, and it isn't there. What can't be ruled out — anything under 2 bps — is smaller than the spread, which makes it undetectable and unprofitable at the same time. More history is not the binding constraint.
